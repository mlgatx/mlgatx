Exercise Caution and Practice Humility: Kenny Lake’s Apriorism on Communist Experience Abroad

October 26, 2026 - David from California
Guest Article
Theoretical
“…the Social-Democratic movement is in its very essence an international movement. This means, not only that we must combat national chauvinism, but that an incipient movement in a young country can be successful only if it makes use of the experiences of other countries. In order to make use of these experiences it is not enough merely to be acquainted with them, or simply to copy out the latest resolutions. What is required is the ability to treat those experiences critically and to test them independently. He who realizes how enormously the modern worker's movement has grown and branched out will understand what a reserve of theoretical forces and political (as well as revolutionary) experience is required to carry out this task.” What is to be Done?, Lenin

In the context of the ideological struggle against economism and narrow thinking, Lenin made this clear assertion for the importance of revolutionary theory and the necessity that a young movement in one country take what it can from experiences abroad. Lenin was also making clear that this process had been occurring already by the very international character of the proletarian struggles. Marx, Engels, and the leaders of the first generation in the European movement had studied closely and taken lessons from each other's countries. As Engels summed up: "the practical German labor movement ought never to forget that it has developed on the shoulders of the English and French movements."

It has been further made clear by more than a century of revolutionary victories that were based on the "experiences of other countries." The Paris Commune taught the world the possibility of proletarian revolution and the importance of consolidating state power, setting the stage for the next century of socialist revolution. The Great October Revolution taught the world about the importance of building a strong party -- in touch with the masses, capable of legal and illegal work, and led by professional revolutionaries. This lesson helped parties around the world to successfully overthrow their bourgeois or at least wage mass resistance to fascism. The Chinese Revolution successfully completed its national-democratic revolution led by the proletariat, deepening the grasp of how the peasants' agrarian revolution could be made a revolutionary force, inspiring young revolutionaries across Asia, Africa, and Latin America. So Lenin was and still is correct -- the experiences and lessons can hold weight beyond one's own borders.

But Lenin places some important caveats on this theoretical task of reaching across borders. According to him, "it is not enough merely to be acquainted with [experiences from abroad], or simply to copy out the latest resolutions." In other words, a student of revolution cannot just read a few books and copy-paste what they have read.

This follows from some common sense. Even if we are not acquainted with revolution, we know how complex an organization or a movement can be. Let's say we found success in founding and organizing a study circle among a group of radical students fresh out of a campus struggle for lower tuition. We know they are already sympathetic to our ideas and a good few of them are well-read in political science. We are able to do this within a few months of work among these students. With it established, we move on. We find a concentration of day-laborers waiting for work on a corner. We think -- this is the working class! Of course they will listen to our ideas. We can do this in at least a few months time. But we run into problems immediately -- we don't speak their language fluently, they are not well-versed in political ideas of any kind, and they are not fresh out of struggle. They just want to work, so they don't talk to us and tell their friends to start frequenting another corner. We were only barely acquainted with organizing students, and became arrogant. We tried to copy-paste our plan, and expected similar results. We are out of luck.

A lazy copy-paste from a revolutionary experience abroad is much the same. To superficially understand the revolutionary experience is to fail to appreciate the particular nature of struggle here, to fail to wage practical struggles amongst the people, and to fail to forward anything worthwhile. It is not equivalent to the process Lenin described, but simply reciting quotations. Examples may come to mind of one or another group quoting Marx, Lenin, or Mao, without really forwarding any explanation for why and how these experiences might apply to our US context. Our friends who quote Gonzalo ad nauseum, without really being connected to any organized masses, really show how easy it is to be merely acquainted with abroad.

These are unserious examples, but this mistake Lenin describes has larger consequences if committed by a serious organization. So let's take an example from the history of the Chinese Revolution. Primarily armed with the Russian experience and influence within the Party, Li Li-san advocated for a plan of immediate armed insurrections, uncoupled from the relatively low organized base. He argued that these city uprisings would swell and inspire revolutionary fervor across China. While Li Li-san's line was only dominant in the Party from June through September 1930 and he stepped down, Chen Shao-yu and Chin Pang-hsien stepped into the gap and re-formulated Li Li-san's line of insurrectionism and military adventurism. The Party and Army was led to countless defeats over four years. Each leading figure referenced the history of the October Revolution to lend their adventurism credibility. The line was a terrible example of copying the image of past revolutionary experience abroad without really analyzing its applicability. Further, as Mao and others would argue, it was missing a clear explanation of how it conformed with the Chinese conditions. Mao and others had already closely studied the peasant movement and identified it as a necessary component of Chinese class society and military strategy. In this situation, the "experiences" taken from abroad were used to justify an erroneous strategy.

So Lenin had said, revolutionaries must "treat experiences critically." Now what could this mean? What does it mean to critically examine something? It would be a good start to analyze it dialectically -- considering the class and political forces at play, the historical context, and all tactics by the victorious party that were put into play. It's a theoretical task, necessary to properly place each hard-fought experience of the proletariat in its role in guiding our struggle here, today. It requires care and caution to not overstate, but to convince the revolutionaries through proof that this -- this right here, should be our path forward. We do ourselves and the masses no favors by taking a shallow pass at a revolutionary time in which thousands or millions put their lives on the line. A successful strategy or tactic in one place, at one time, might lead to lives lost needlessly in another. Taking these stakes seriously means that every strategy, whether represented in polemic or battle plan, should be held to the upmost scrutiny. There's no room for know-it-alls who know very little.

As communists in the United States conduct practical work, study, and share views with one another towards assembling a party-building network, this attitude of caution, scrutiny, and humility is absolutely necessary. Groups and circles have routinely presented very shallow analysis on the general lessons of the communist movement abroad, on the application of those lessons here, and how those might be practically accomplished. What has passed for analysis within the American "Maoist" movement has either been simple regurgitation of Gonzalo's speeches with little new content, or piecemeal and eclectic musing on revolutionary movements abroad outside of its context. Neither of these are analysis that we need.

"The most ridiculous person in the world is the "know all" who picks up a smattering of hearsay knowledge and proclaims himself "the world's Number One authority"; this merely shows that he has not taken a proper measure of himself. Knowledge is a matter of science, and no dishonesty or conceit whatsoever is permissible. What is required is definitely the reverse--honesty and modesty." - On Practice, Mao Zedong

Let's take an example that is both more serious and more sophisticated than the Gonzaloites. The Organization of Communist Revolutionaries (OCR) is a group in the US that has authored a sizable body of theoretical work and propaganda across two journals. From where I'm standing, not too many other organizations have publicly addressed them or their work, though many are talking about them and making sense of the efforts they inspire. This here is not an exhaustive review of their work and their heterodox positions, but others should engage with them and pick apart the good and the bad. Undoubtedly there is some value in their research, experience, and ongoing work.

Within the larger piece The Spectre that Still Haunts, the OCR puts forward a few main theses:

  1. The proletariat has historically, and rather mechanically, been taken to be equivalent only to the industrial working-class -- particularly among revisionist parties in imperialist countries. But it is the proletariat's dispossession, not its concentration in workplaces that better defines it in relation to its society and its revolutionary outlook. And it is in these processes of dispossession that people are the most receptive to revolution.
  2. Major changes have wrought the capitalist-imperialist system in the last 50 years, which have changed the conditions in both imperialist and oppressed countries. Namely, these are a growing slum population of dispossessed proletarians across the world and major migrations creating immigrant proletarians in imperialist countries.
  3. Drawn from the major processes of mass incarceration, migration from oppressed countries, and gentrification, the two major sections of the proletariat in the US today are Black proletarians in the criminal system and immigrant proletarians working service, agriculture, and sometimes industrial jobs.

The piece is ambitious, and at this point over ten years old. Whether or not it fulfills its overall mission is beyond the scope of this essay. The main point to tackle here is how the OCR handles the task of analyzing experiences abroad. It is Part 3 of Spectre which is at issue here, called "When We Ride On Our Enemies". In it, Kenny Lake sets out to test the thesis, through an analysis of people's wars, that:

"it is in the process of dispossession, including the casting off of people into the reserve army of labor, and through the volatile effects of the social anarchy of capitalist production that masses of people most receptive to the aims of and immediate need for communist revolution can be found."

Part 3 consists of three treatments of the people's wars in Peru, the Philippines, and India. However, they are not full summations of each war's line and practice, but treatments of specific regions of each war, in specific times -- Lima, Davao City, and the Dandakaranya forest region. In addition to attempting to prove the central thesis, there are moments within the piece where the author suggests (if not asserts) some other takeaway. For example, Lake muses mid-essay that a Paris Commune-style event may be necessary to inspire the resurgence of revolution globally.

The piece's main sources are journalistic and academic writings on each people's war, some being sympathetic and some antagonistic to the aims of each war. Lake suggests that there should be some preference for this rather than simply taking as-is the summations of the respective communist parties, due to a lack of thorough description in those summations. And as Lenin said, revolutionaries should "test [experiences] independently."

The problems in this piece and the lessons we should draw first begin at this initial framework. There is a theoretical thesis that Lake wants to prove. Lake chooses a few periods of revolutionary upsurge in regions of the last 50 years to prove said thesis. Then, Lake chooses to omit certain types of primary and secondary sources.

The very framework of taking individual cities or regions as sole objects of analysis is a choice, both argumentatively and ideologically. The author makes a choice to focus on these places rather than others. Why were these places chosen by our author? Well, we can safely assume that the narratives told will be utilized to bolster the thesis Lake set out to prove. There are undoubtedly other facts that can be used for explanation -- Lima was and is the largest city in Peru and the Dandakaranya forest region was the primary site for revolutionary activity in India for some time. But it would be good for us to remember that none of these places are equivalent to the entire country in which the revolution was taking place. Why is this important? And isn't there something general to be found in the particular, as Mao said? Well yes -- but there is also something general to be found in the national and international experience. With more data, we might be able to better find and identify what is really good in a given strategy, and what may lead to problems. But if we pick one area, and try to extrapolate lessons from there only, we will in fact miss the ways tactics in one place affect the larger situation.

In the context of intra-Party debates about the Disorientation of the 1980s, certain individuals and committees within the Communist Party of the Philippines (CPP) used this very city-based form of analysis to forward certain opinions. The Central Committee explains here how this necessarily becomes tied up in an ideological position:

"Instead of having a comprehensive, complete and all-sided view of things and theoretical development from a proletarian revolutionary stand, there is a narrow, one-sided and fragmentary view of these [experiences], depending on which deviation certain elements wish to promote. For instance, there are elements who exaggerate the current role of their urban area of work and eclectically take out of historical context certain dramatic events, like the Petrograd and Moscow uprisings, the Vietnamese uprising of 1945, the Tet offensive of 1968 and the Nicaraguan final offensive of 1979 in order to insist on the “autonomous/specific dynamism of urban struggle” (apart from the entire strategy) and devise a “new strategy.” - Documents of the Second Great Rectification Movement, Communist Party of the Philippines

So while it may be presented as humbleness to not attempt to take stock of a whole revolution, the narrowing of scope necessarily means emphasizing some facts and omitting others. In the context of the people's war in the Philippines, the danger was that notable cadres were really questioning the validity of the primacy of the countryside and the role of the peasantry. In a country where the majority were still peasants, this trend became tied up in a number of "Left" and Right deviations for quick victory -- premature company and battalion formation in the NPA, premature formation of Armed Partisans in the cities, and the ability of solely mass protests to topple the government. As we will explore further, this apparently led to major issues -- decrease of mass base, security issues of cadres, and mass hysteria.

We are obviously not waging a people's war, but what is being implied in this piece is to consider these tactics. And if we are considering these tactics, it is only fair to see that whole picture. What is at issue is the form of analysis that narrows its scope to fit its agenda. This is easy to justify when talking about examples from abroad, due to the scarcity of information, but nonetheless short-sighted. Rather than take overseas experience in its context, it removes that context and all the inconvenient errors that disprove the thesis.

The second issue comes from the choice to emphasize independent journalistic and academic writings over the summations and writings of each communist party. Lake explains the rationale:

"Within the international communist movement the process of summation has too often focused one-sidedly on the documents and resolutions of the communist parties involved rather than the practices of those revolutions. This relates to a larger weakness of Maoism: while Maoists, or at least those who are Maoist in deed and not just in declaration, have been exemplary at carrying out detailed social investigation among the basic masses and using concrete analysis of concrete conditions to guide strategy and practice, on a theoretical level Maoists have been far too content to issue general statements and rehash general principles rather than rigorously developing theory on the basis of practical experience and empirical data... Given the above critique of existing Maoist discourse, this analysis seeks to draw theoretical lessons largely from empirical data rather than the documents and resolutions of communist parties."

Such an assertion, in my eyes, requires proof. Unfortunately, the essay apparently needed to power ahead and Lake had no time. The troubling thing is that there are two major collections of documents that largely cover the periods covered in Part 3, these being Political and Organization Review for the 9th Unity Congress by CPI (Maoist) and the Documents of the Second Great Rectification Movement by CPP. I'm doubtful that Lake is unaware of these collections, which are hundreds of documents apiece and publicly available online. Lake even cites in Footnote 96 a document from the SGRM, and comments:

"Unlike the trend of communist party documents and resolutions content to make general proclamations, this document is exemplary at digging into the particulars of strategy, tactics, and organization and bringing to life the ramifications of “line” in revolutionary practice."

It's exemplary! But Lake applies the same method of analysis to Davao, favoring journalistic accounts over the documents by the party. All citations of facts apart from the one above come from two journalistic accounts. Lake's argument may be applicable to some, or even a majority of parties across the world. But it should give us pause when there are major counter-examples to the assertion Lake makes here. So why does Lake do it?

To me it is clear that Lake largely disagrees with the conclusions reached by parties abroad in their summations. Lake prefers to present Lake's own conclusions -- and this in it of itself is not incorrect. As Lenin says, revolutionaries should be able to "test independently." But what is an issue is the omission of facts pertaining to results of said tactics and the internal debates within the party tied up in this or that decision. It's essentially a one-sided method of analysis -- presenting an independent viewpoint without due consideration of negative consequences or counter-opinions, which were borne out in history. And while dedicated readers may look into the details and find these counter-examples, its essentially a dishonest omission.

And omissions become substantial when we take stock of Lake's narrative with respect to Davao City against the record included in Documents of the SGRM. What follows is a review about the tactics and strategy that were employed in Davao City and Mindanao, comparing Lake's account with the CPP's assessment among the Documents of the SGRM. We will see that there are substantial differences, and from that we will be able to draw out a few cautionary lessons in analysis of experiences abroad.

From the jump, Lake begins with accounting the ways that Davao City's population ballooned among the urban slums. Settlers from across the archipelago converged on Mindanao's promise of arable land but were largely unable and forced into Davao City's slums and nearby rural villages. It's a description of circumstances in line with the thesis Lake sought to prove with this piece: that the sharp and rapid migration into Davao was utilized by the CPP to rapidly grow the revolutionary movement.

From these objective conditions, Lake essentially forwards the position that the choice of the CPP, under objective conditions in Davao, to form the Armed City Partisans and wage confrontational mass actions were a mostly-positive endeavor. Lake marshals facts solely from journalistic accounts from Chapman and Jones that show growth -- growth in mass base, growth in CPP members, growth in the number of confrontational actions, and growth in the number of assassination activities. Further, this armed activity took place side-by-side with legal actions in the city such as the formation of trade unions. And due to these objective conditions, the CPP/NPA in Mindanao chose to begin organizing its Armed City Partisan units. When we are only looking at these facts, it's extremely compelling that Davao was a hotbed for revolutionary activity and for a time, these were largely commendable and correct tactics to employ.

It is certainly possible to suggest, as Lake does, that the rapid growth of Davao's slums provided a convincing reason for a focus towards urban work and the formation of Armed City Partisans. However, the CPP ascribes several more reasons, largely internal and theoretical, for the growth of urban work and the formation of the ACP not just in Davao, but in other urban centers of the Philippines. Beginning in the early 1980s, the central leadership of the CPP had already begun making tactical decisions to focus on urban work. Reasons thrown around at the time included new views on the level of urbanization in the country, the underdevelopment of urban work and broad alliance work, and the strength of the rural armed movement. This led to a stop to the deployment of cadres and activists from the cities to the countryside, which had been the backbone of the growth in armed and political strength of the people's war in the previous decade.

All these tactical decisions became formalized with the concept of the "strategic counteroffensive" -- adopted by the Politbureau in 1981, affirmed by the 9th Central Committee Plenum, and withdrawn in 1990 as the Second Great Rectification Movement sought to correct past errors. Resting on the premise that victory was near, central leadership emphasized city-based legal and illegal work (such as Armed City Partisan units) alongside regularization of the NPA into larger units. It was essentially an insurrectionist strategy.

Insurrections are a part of the strategy of people's war, and clearly necessary in all armed confrontations with capitalist or semi-colonial states, as borne out by history. However, history also confirms that not all insurrections are successful and it is largely the organized strength of the masses which dictates victory or defeat. This is what distinguishes an adventurist position from a correct one when both are considering insurrection. In the case of the CPP in the 1980s, the strategic counter-offensive was divorced from key questions like: What is the strength of our mass base in the countryside and in the cities? What is the strength of our army? How do these measure up against the enemy? What is required to sustain and grow our forces?

As is explained in Reaffirm Our Basic Principles and Rectify Errors, the Mindanao Commission adopted the strategic counter offensive strategies to the greatest extent and therefore faced some of its most dramatic consequences. To Lake's credit, they include a paragraph at the end of their essay that describes the differences in line between the Mindanao Commission and the later Central Committee of the CPP. But it's really only hedging against their clear affinity to mistaken tactics that they call "the revolutionary advances in Davao City in the first half of the 1980s." Lake gushes over the combination of legal fronts, ACP armed propaganda and assassinations, and focus on the cities. But much is missed as far as downstream effects. These effects are covered in detail in the documents of the Second Great Rectification Movement.

On building the mass movement, Lake really washes over the contradictions inherent in combined legal and illegal (armed) activity in the cities. In doing so, they fail to appreciate the particularities of both. The legal movement is really a defensive movement, meant to organize the broadest masses of people for democratic and economic rights. Within and alongside it is the illegal activity -- party-work, party propaganda, intelligence, and armed activity. An example of a mistaken combination is the welgang bayan strategy that Lake gushes over. As Reaffirm details, they were considered "a process of building up towards popular uprisings," combining mass actions with increasingly confrontational elements and open support of the CPP. So, they were considered a tactic on the path to insurrection and its success was measured by a yardstick not primarily of mass participation, but confrontation. Often, they were characterized more by shutting down highways than mass rallies -- drawing in crowds of only the advanced. And there lies the problem -- no consideration of the actual capacity of the people to pursue and desire such confrontational tactics at that moment in time. And it had the effect of "heat[ing] up these small cities beyond the capacity of the 'wanted' cadres to conceal themselves."

Leading cadres involved both in these legal mass actions and insurrectionary activity were not easy to hide in small, basically rural cities. Leaders in Mindanao had to flee to nearby provinces, and military checkpoints choked the movement of remaining activists. Unfortunately, comrades in Mindanao responded with one of the worst episodes of hysteria in Philippines history. Based on faulty testimony, leading cadres of Mindanao believed there was a widespread problem of infiltrators within the Party, army, and mass organizations, and so began arresting suspects. They considered these saboteurs to be the primary cause of the already-diminishing mass base by 1985. Reaffirm gives the following statistics of its effects from early 1980s to the latter half:

  1. Close to 1,000 people became victims of civil rights violations and severe punishment
  2. Party membership fell from 9,000 to 3,000
  3. The mass base shrank by 50%
  4. The 15 companies and 30 platoons were reduced to 2 companies and 17 platoons

On military matters, Lake utters the truest sentence of the essay when they say, "the NPA was not in the position to defend urban neighborhoods." Lake desperately tries to keep the question open of how revolutionary violence is sustained in urban areas, as the question is answered again and again. A party really cannot sustain it against a superior enemy. So it must grow a large and defensive legal movement, build a broad underground, and gather experience through armed actions that both are supported by the masses and conform with the organized movement. But Lake really does not appreciate the sophistication of protracted people's war or the criteria by which Lenin measures a revolutionary situation. Clearly, they have passion for ending the suffering of the masses, but to do so also means to move with the masses as much as it means to move the masses. Lake repeats the mistaken view that the NPA was expanding into platoon and company-sized units appropriately in the countryside. But Lake again ignores the actual experience. The expanded military fronts had initial success in winning against the enemy and confiscating weapons. But the military soon regrouped and sent greater numbers of soldiers, and the NPA platoons and companies were unable to beat back those encirclements. Further, more time in purely military activities meant less time as guerilla units engaging in production work and mass work amongst the peasantry. And when a large company came to camp by a village, could the sympathetic peasantry even feed all those NPA soldiers? And so the mass base fell off a cliff in the latter half of the 80s.

The gulf between Lake's account and the CPP's should give any reader pause. It presents some good lessons on ways that activists abroad may err in offering one-sided analysis of experiences abroad. We have seen, through negative example, ways in which those abroad may either not be privy to or ignore key facts within a revolutionary experience. Lake's method of analysis is essentially apriorism, choosing facts to fit an already-decided thesis. It is the same method that Engels criticized Duhring for.

To attempt to analyze an experience requires a thorough appreciation of all facts. Tactics, strategy, and experiences should be framed within the historical development and current relations of classes within the national context. Failing to do so necessarily means ignoring key reasons or consequences of an experience. It should not narrow the scope of analysis to a smaller place or time so as to ignore inconvenient facts.

Documents of the revolutionary forces should factor in as a large source of facts and analysis, if not the primary source when summations exist. Parties are often operating with a large body of practical experience that has been filtered, analyzed, and agreed upon by hundreds, if not thousands of people. As Lenin said, "He who realizes how enormously the modern worker's movement has grown and branched out will understand what a reserve of theoretical forces and political (as well as revolutionary) experience is required to carry out this task."

When discussing or advocating for illegal or armed revolutionary activity, the stakes become all the more serious for a thorough, all-around discussion of the positive and negative outcomes. To one-sidedly take the positive or ignore the implications in overall strategy is to act irresponsibly and invite adventurism or capitulation. It prematurely exposes the revolutionary forces to slaughter and may cause runaway consequences. Alternatively, to take only the negative is to ignore the conditions in which armed activity has been able to be sustained -- in acute revolutionary situations of war or crisis, or in the underdeveloped countryside. The forces that argue otherwise have really not been able to prove their theses and have relied on an omission of historical facts.

The lessons from abroad that have bred success are those which have been carefully and fully theorized and aligned with concrete conditions. We owe nothing less to ourselves and the massive tasks ahead of us.
